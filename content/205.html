The Traffic Pattern

Published 11-21-2010


Chapter 5 The Traffic Pattern


The WHAT IS and the WHY FOR of a Traffic Pattern.


The Civilian Traffic Pattern consists of five elements, upwind leg, crosswind leg, downwind leg, base leg, and final approach. These elements are common to all traffic patterns (even the Overhead Approach), and only differ in the direction of the turns being made as you fly the pattern. Left hand turns are the default direction of a traffic pattern.



The Military Traffic Pattern (often called the Overhead Approach or Flameout Approach) consists of three elements, initial, break, and rollout. Both patterns are designed for one purpose, and that is to set the airplane up for landing. Each element has a corresponding aircraft configuration and speed.



That's the WHAT IS of a Traffic Pattern, now the WHY FOR. In terms of landing at an airport, airbase, landing strip, or whatever name you choose, the intention or purpose is a terminating point for a flight, or any number of flights arriving at nearly the same time. With a single ship flight little if any planning is required to safely maneuver for a landing. However, when you have multiple flights approaching an airfield there has to be some sort of built in separation and traffic control.

When a single ship or multiple ship flight is approaching an airfield that has many hundreds of feet of concrete, and is visible from many miles away, the standard approach is that of the Overhead Approach. Refer to the illustration and note that the approach begins with the initial approach segment at approximately 3 to 5 miles from the end of the runway. Your altitude should be between 1500 ft and 2000 ft. It is while on initial that you begin to reduce you speed, slowing to around 200 mph as you reach the break point. Upon reaching the break point, (which is the runway threshold if a single ship or the lead airplane of a multiple ship flight), you further reduce your speed and begin a descending 180 turn to the left. As your speed decreases below 200 mph you can lower the first notch of flaps. After completing 180 degrees of turn, and speed below 180 mph, you can lower your landing gear while continuing to descend. With the landing gear down and your before landing check complete, lower the second notch of flaps and start another 180 degree turn and roll out on runway heading. At this point your speed should be around 140 mph and you should put in the last notch of flaps. Adjust you pitch and power to cross the runway threshold at 120 mph and power off for the landing.

In a multiple ship flight, everybody can't break at the same time, so each airplane must extent its initial approach at a 1 to 2 second interval after the lead airplane breaks. This effectively moves the break point down the runway and makes it necessary for each subsequent ship in the flight to fly a slightly larger pattern. This will require that you maintain altitude while in the first 180 degrees of turn and depending on your position in the flight, maintaining altitude until you are abeam the runway threshold. Just like the lead airplane, you will extend your first notch of flaps in the first 180 degree turn and your landing gear on the downwind leg. Once abeam the runway threshold, begin the second 180 degree turn and lower the second notch of flaps and start your descent. As you roll out on final extend the last notch of flaps and adjust pitch and power to cross the runway threshold at 120 mph and power off for the landing.

The above scenario represents a single ship flight or a flight of more than one airplane. If a number of airplanes are approaching the airfield, (other than in a flight), it can become very chaotic unless there is a set standard for entering the traffic pattern. First of all, any aircraft in distress has the right-of-way over all other aircraft and it is immaterial as to how they get on the ground. However, when a number of aircraft are approaching at the same time you must first determine the runway of intended landing and merge with other aircraft in the pattern.

With other than a concrete runway it can be very difficult to see and determine which runway would be suitable for landing. In this case over fly the airfield at an altitude of between 2000 and 2500 ft. When over the airfield determine the direction of landing and maneuver so as to enter on the downwind leg. At this point descend to your pattern altitude of around 1500 to 2000 ft. Prior to entering the downwind, you should have reduced your airspeed to around 200 mph and lowered you first notch of flaps. When established on downwind the gear should be extended and speed further reduced. After turning base another notch of flaps are lowered, and turning final will require the last notch of flaps and cross the runway threshold at 120 mph power off and complete the landing. The goal here is to always be on downwind at the same speed, altitude, and aircraft configuration. If you can accomplish this, then every and I do mean every landing will be the same. This is what is considered a stabilized approach.

Now what do you do if you are one of many aircraft approaching the airfield at the same time. If the direction of landing has been established and other aircraft are in the pattern, then you want to maneuver to a point wide of the downwind leg and enter the downwind leg at a 45 degree angle. Be sure that you don't cut anybody off and that your speed has been reduced to match that of others in the pattern. If you find that the spacing doesn't allow for you to merge, then remain above the pattern altitude and fly the pattern without descending until you can safely merge your airplane with the others. This may mean that you have to fly the traffic pattern (above pattern altitude), for several circuits until you can descent and merge with other aircraft. It is preferable that when you fly the pattern you remain relatively close to the airfield and not let the pattern get strung out over a large area.

When to opportunity allows you to descend and merge with the other airplanes in the pattern, you should have your aircraft configured on the downwind with the first notch of flaps, and the gear down. Once again, this is how we want to be configured for the stabilized approach. From downwind leg down to landing the approach is always the same resulting in crossing the runway threshold at 120 mph and power off for the landing. After you touch down on the runway, let the airplane rollout with out without slowing down and forcing aircraft behind you to go around. Additionally land on one side of the runway or the other depending on the aircraft if front of you. That way aircraft landing behind you will have the option of clearing your aircraft to the left or right as appropriate.

Little if any communications are required when flying the appropriate traffic pattern. It isn't necessary to communicate you position if you are over flying the airfield waiting to merge with other aircraft. The first position report you would be required to make would be reporting on downwind. Example: "RamblinJack on downwind". What that means is that I am on downwind with my airplane configured for landing, and I plan on turning base leg and final as appropriate and landing. It doesn't mean I am circling overhead or waiting to merge or anything else. It means I am configured for landing and if not the first airplane in the pattern, I am following the airplane in front of me. By reporting your position on downwind you are telling other aircraft of you intentions. That way if others are following they will know that you are set up for the landing and they can plan accordingly. The next position report would be "RamblinJack turning base", and the final position report would be "RamblinJack turning final". A total of nine words are used to convey the information needed for you to inform other aircraft and get yourself on the ground. Nine words, can you image this group doing anything with only nine words.

So let's recap:

  • The traffic pattern is designed to configure the aircraft for landing and provide an orderly way of accommodating many aircraft at one time.
    		•
  • Enter the traffic pattern at the appropriate speed (200 mph).
    		•
  • When approaching the airfield over fly the prescribed pattern at an altitude above other aircraft (2000 to 2500 ft.), and merge with other aircraft without cutting anyone off or creating an undue conflict.
    		•
  • By configuring the airplane the same way at the same point (usually on the downwind), we set up for a stabilized approach and the landing is the same every time.
    		•
  • Fly a tight pattern and don't let the different pattern legs get strung out.
    		•
  • Report on downwind only when you are configured for landing, and if you are not the number one aircraft, you have those if front in sight and plan on following.
    		•
  • After landing continue the rollout so as not to force aircraft behind you to go around.
    		•
